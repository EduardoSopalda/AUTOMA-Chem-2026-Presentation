# From Brasília to the Plant Floor
## Speaking script, cut version for ten minutes

Eduardo Sopalda. AUTOMA Chem 2026, Berlin, 26 October.

---

### 00. OPENING

There's a question on the screen. Keep it in mind. I'll come back to it at the end.

---

### 01. THE ARCHITECT

I am an architect. Not as a metaphor. I studied architecture. I drew buildings.

Then I spent the last twenty years designing something else: data and AI governance for large organisations. Today I lead governance across every business unit at dsm-firmenich, sustainability included.

I moved from designing physical spaces to designing the structures around data. And I keep seeing the same problem. We design from above, not for the person who has to live with it.

Sustainability is full of ambitious plans, targets and sophisticated technology. But every one of them eventually has to meet a human being. Someone has to extract the data, interpret it, challenge it, trust it, and sometimes tell the system that it is wrong.

I work inside a chemical company, like many of you, and I have got plenty of these things wrong myself. What I want to explore is the distance between the beautiful plan and what actually happens at ground level.

---

### 02. BRASÍLIA

Let me take you to Brasília. One of the most extraordinary examples of designing from above.

From the air it is monumental, precise and unforgettable. Jan Gehl gave a name to what happens when plans are drawn looking down and dropped onto the ground: the Brasília Syndrome. A city can make perfect sense from a helicopter and feel completely different when you walk through it.

The distances are enormous. There are very few of the small spaces where people actually meet. The point is scale. A plan can be beautiful and still fail the person standing inside it.

And there is one detail I want you to sit with. Many of the people who built Brasília could not afford to live in the city they had built. They commuted in from satellite towns.

The city was finished. It just was not designed around the people who had to live and work in it.

---

### 03. WHEN THE DRAWING MEETS THE GROUND

We build governance in a very similar way. Policies, controls, committees, operating models and, occasionally, a beautiful RACI that makes everyone feel that civilisation has finally arrived. Seen from above, everything looks symmetrical and complete.

Then the framework reaches the plant.

The policy is too long to cross. The process is too wide to walk through on a Tuesday morning when the plant is running. And there is no space where a process engineer and a data owner discover that they have used the same word to mean two different things for three years.

I have been the person with the beautiful drawing. I have presented the framework, watched people nod, and then watched them carry on exactly as before. They were not being difficult. I had built them a plaza nobody wanted to stand in.

So I stopped asking whether a framework was complete. I started asking whether people actually used it.

---

### 04. SUSTAINABILITY HAS THE SAME PROBLEM

Sustainability has the same problem. We are very good at designing it from above. Ambitions, targets, roadmaps. Emissions, sourcing, circularity, footprints.

But eventually the ambition has to become evidence.

At dsm-firmenich, sustainability is expressed through People, Planet and Progress. I like that, because it puts people inside the story, not outside it. The ambition may be global, but the data that makes it measurable is created across businesses, processes, systems, suppliers and people.

So how do you make sure that information still means the same thing when it reaches the other end?

A sustainability number is only as strong as what stands underneath it. Where did it come from? Who owns it? What definition sits behind it? What happens when two systems disagree? And who is prepared to stand behind it when somebody asks a difficult question?

That is where sustainability and governance stop being two separate conversations.

---

### 05. THE CARBON NUMBER

A carbon number looks like chemistry. It is not. It is a data product.

Behind one simple number sit supplier declarations, energy meters, batch records, transport data, allocation rules and assumptions. Much of it comes from systems never designed to be added together, and from suppliers who use their own definitions.

So the challenge is not calculating the number. The challenge is explaining it.

Once it becomes part of reporting and assurance, lineage, ownership and shared definitions stop being data hygiene. They become evidence. Someone has to know which source is right. Someone has to explain the result to a person who was not in the room when the decision was made.

You cannot report what you cannot trace.

---

### 06. WHY DON'T WE JUST USE AI?

And then, inevitably, someone says: why don't we just use AI?

I am optimistic about AI. But we sometimes ask it to solve problems that existed long before it arrived.

If five people use the word batch to mean eight different things, putting an AI agent on top does not remove the ambiguity. It scales it.

In one agent project I saw, around ten thousand documents went in. Roughly two thousand were relevant. The rest were duplicates, superseded drafts, the same deck under four different filenames. All of it was embedded. All of it consumed tokens and energy.

So filtering before you embed is not just an AI optimisation. It is a governance decision, a cost decision and a sustainability decision.

And sometimes the answer is simply agreeing on what a word means before we ask a machine to interpret it.

---

### 07. THE ROAD TO THE SHINY CITY

Everyone wants the shiny city on the hill. The autonomous plant, the perfect digital twin, AI agents that help us decide at speed.

But we do not get there by teleportation.

The road to that city is paved with data. Not the glamorous kind. Definitions, ownership, lineage, quality and context. And people who know what a number actually means.

If that road is built from data nobody trusts, definitions nobody shares and processes nobody adopts, the city on the hill does not matter. We have simply built another Brasília.

---

### 08. BACK AT GROUND LEVEL

Take digital twins. The interesting question is not what a digital twin is. It is whether anyone believes it.

AI can help build and run these models. It can find patterns and predict behaviour. And a good twin shows where energy is consumed, where material is lost, where emissions are generated. That is when sustainability becomes part of how we operate, not something we measure afterwards.

But the twin is only as good as what flows into it. If the equipment data is incomplete, if the definition of yield changes between systems, if nobody trusts the model, it is still only a picture on a screen.

It becomes sustainable, in both senses, when the operator on the night shift trusts what it tells them. When the engineer knows which definition of yield sits behind it. And when somebody is accountable if it is wrong.

That is why governance has to be designed at human scale. If the person using the system does not understand it, trust it, or know what to do when it is wrong, we have not transformed the process. We have added technology to it.

Agents do not own risk. They do not carry accountability.

Humans do. Always.

---

### 09. BACK TO BRASÍLIA

So let me take you back to Brasília. The city is still there. Still beautiful from the air.

The real question is whether we can build sustainable digital transformation without losing the people who have to make it work.

I think we can. And I think it is worth doing.

Frameworks are necessary. They are just not sufficient. The opportunity is to build systems that are useful, trusted and sustainable in practice.

---

### CODA. THE QUESTION

So here is the question from the start.

What is one word in your organisation that means something different depending on which floor you are standing on?

Ask it at lunch. The answer may tell you more about your governance than another hundred pages of framework ever could.

Thank you.
